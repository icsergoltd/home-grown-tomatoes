A tomato plant that suddenly droops can look as if it is dying, but wilting does not always mean disaster. **Tomato plants wilting** in the middle of a hot afternoon are often reacting to heat or temporary water stress. If the plant perks up by evening or the next morning, the problem is usually manageable. If it stays limp, yellows, collapses, or wilts even when the soil is moist, look more closely for root damage, drainage problems, disease, pests or chemical injury.

**Quick answer:** check the soil first. If it is dry several centimetres down, water slowly and deeply at the base of the plant. If the soil is already wet, do not keep adding water. Instead, inspect the stem, roots, leaves and growing conditions. Persistent wilting can be caused by waterlogged roots, Fusarium or Verticillium wilt, bacterial wilt, southern blight, root-knot nematodes, stalk borers, walnut toxicity, tomato spotted wilt virus, herbicide damage or severe heat stress.

## Quick diagnosis: what kind of wilt is it?

Use this table to narrow down the likely cause before you act. The most useful clues are **when the plant wilts**, **whether it recovers overnight**, **what the soil feels like**, and **whether the leaves are yellowing, distorted or still green**.

| What you see | Most likely cause | What to check next | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plant droops in hot sun but recovers by evening or morning | Heat stress or temporary water stress | Soil moisture, mulch, wind exposure, container size | Water deeply if dry, shade during extreme heat, mulch the soil |
| Whole plant limp and soil is dry | Drought stress | Dry compost or soil several centimetres down | Water slowly at the base until the root zone is evenly moist |
| Plant wilts although soil is wet | Waterlogged roots, root disease or vascular wilt | Drainage, sour-smelling compost, stem discolouration | Stop watering, improve drainage, inspect for disease signs |
| Lower leaves yellow, then wilt; one side may be worse | Fusarium wilt or Verticillium wilt | Brown streaking inside lower stem | Remove badly affected plants; use resistant varieties in future |
| Plant wilts rapidly with leaves staying green | Bacterial wilt or severe root/stem damage | Cut-stem water test, lower stem symptoms | Remove plant and seek local diagnosis if bacterial wilt is suspected |
| Stem collapses at soil line with white fungal growth or tan seed-like bodies | Southern blight | Base of stem and soil surface | Remove plant and surrounding debris; avoid moving contaminated soil |
| Plants are stunted, pale and wilt easily in patches | Root-knot nematodes | Knobbly galls on roots | Remove plant after cropping; rotate, use clean compost or resistant varieties |
| One stem or plant wilts suddenly; small hole in stem | Stalk borer | Entry hole, tunnelling, caterpillar | Remove affected stem or plant; control nearby tall weeds |
| Bronzed leaves, stunting and rings on fruit | Tomato spotted wilt virus | Thrips presence, uneven leaf colour, fruit rings | Remove infected plants; manage weeds and choose resistant varieties where needed |
| Twisted, narrow, strap-like new growth | Herbicide injury | Nearby lawn weedkiller, contaminated compost or clippings | Remove chemical source; wait for clean new growth if damage is mild |

If your plant is also showing widespread yellowing, compare the symptoms with [why tomato leaves turn yellow](/blog/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-leaves-turning-yellow/). Yellowing plus wilt often points to a different problem than simple heat droop.

## Start with the soil moisture test

![A hand checking soil moisture 5–8cm deep beside a tomato plant.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-2-soil-moisture-test.webp)




Before diagnosing disease, do the simple test that solves many wilting cases.

1. Push a finger, trowel or moisture probe 5–8cm into the soil near the root zone.
2. Check more than one spot, especially with large plants or containers.
3. If the soil is dry at that depth, water slowly and deeply.
4. If the soil is moist or soggy, do not add more water until you have checked drainage and roots.

Tomatoes have a fairly shallow, fibrous root system compared with deep-rooted shrubs, so the upper root zone matters. Dry soil near the surface can make young plants wilt quickly, but constantly wet soil can be just as damaging because roots need oxygen as well as moisture.

A good target for garden tomatoes is steady moisture rather than a strict daily routine. Many in-ground plants need roughly 2.5cm of water per week in ordinary summer weather, but this changes with soil type, rainfall, heat, wind, plant size and mulch. Containers and growbags may need watering once a day or more in hot weather.

## Heat stress and temporary afternoon wilting

![Tomato leaves drooping in strong afternoon sun, suggesting temporary heat stress.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-3-heat-stress-afternoon.webp)




Tomatoes often droop during the hottest part of the day because the leaves are losing water faster than the roots can replace it. This is especially common during heatwaves, in windy sites, against hot walls, in black containers, or after a sudden change from mild to hot weather.

Heat-stressed tomatoes may show:

- limp leaves in strong afternoon sun;
- upward leaf rolling or cupping;
- recovery in the evening or by the next morning;
- dry leaf edges if stress continues;
- flower drop or poor fruit set during prolonged heat.

Temporary afternoon wilt is not automatically a crisis. It becomes more serious if the plant remains wilted for many hours, fails to recover overnight, or is also showing yellowing, browning, stem collapse or root damage.

To help a heat-stressed tomato plant:

- water deeply at the base if the soil is dry;
- apply organic mulch, keeping it a little away from the stem;
- move pots to a spot with morning sun and light afternoon shade during extreme heat;
- use shade cloth temporarily rather than shutting out light all day;
- avoid heavy pruning in very hot weather, as leaves help shade fruit and stems;
- wait until cooler conditions before applying fertiliser or sprays unless a product label clearly says it is safe in heat.

If flowers are dropping as well as leaves wilting, high temperatures and plant stress may be involved. See [why tomato flowers fall off](/blog/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-flowers-falling-off/) for more detail on blossom drop.

## Watering problems: too dry, too wet or too erratic

![Watering a tomato at the base with water flowing onto dry soil.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-4-underwatering-deep-water.webp)

![Wilting tomato plant sitting in wet, soggy soil with poor drainage cues.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-5-overwatering-wet-soil.webp)





### Underwatering

Underwatered tomatoes usually wilt from the top and outer leaves first, especially during hot afternoons. The soil feels dry, containers feel light, and leaves may recover quickly after a thorough watering.

Water slowly so the whole root zone becomes moist. A quick splash often wets only the surface and encourages shallow roots. If water runs off dry soil, pause for a few minutes and water again. For containers, water until excess drains from the bottom, then let the compost drain freely.

### Overwatering and poor drainage

A wilted plant in wet soil is a warning sign. Roots deprived of oxygen cannot move water properly, so the plant can droop even though the soil is wet. This often happens in heavy clay, low spots, containers without drainage holes, compacted beds, or growbags sitting in trays of stagnant water.

Signs of overwatering or poor drainage include:

- soil that stays wet for days;
- yellowing lower leaves;
- weak, slow growth;
- sour-smelling compost;
- roots that look brown, mushy or sparse rather than pale and firm.

Stop watering until the top layer begins to dry. Improve drainage where possible by lifting pots off saucers, opening blocked drainage holes, avoiding standing water, and using raised beds in persistently wet ground. Do not try to “feed” a waterlogged plant back to health; fertiliser will not fix roots that cannot breathe.

### Erratic watering

Erratic watering may not always cause obvious wilt, but it weakens tomato plants and contributes to other problems. Letting plants dry out completely and then soaking them heavily can also lead to fruit splitting and calcium-related disorders such as blossom end rot. If you see dark, sunken patches at the bottom of developing fruit, read our guide to [blossom end rot on tomatoes](/blog/tomato-problems/blossom-end-rot-on-tomatoes/).

The aim is not to keep soil constantly wet. The aim is to keep it **evenly moist** through the main root zone.

## Container tomatoes that wilt quickly

![A tomato wilting in a small patio container in hot sun.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-6-container-wilt-pot-size.webp)




Container-grown tomatoes wilt faster than plants in the ground because there is less compost to hold water and roots are more exposed to heat. Hanging baskets, small pots and growbags can dry very quickly once plants are large and carrying fruit.

For beginners, the easiest way to reduce container wilting is to start with enough root space. A compact bush tomato can manage in a smaller pot than a vigorous cordon variety, but larger containers are generally easier to water evenly. A pot of at least 30cm across is a useful minimum for many patio tomatoes, while larger containers give a bigger moisture buffer.

Container checklist:

- Use a container with drainage holes.
- Fill with good-quality, moisture-retentive potting compost.
- Water before the compost becomes dust-dry.
- Feed with tomato fertiliser once fruit begins to form, following the label.
- Mulch the compost surface to reduce evaporation.
- Move pots out of the fiercest afternoon sun during heatwaves.
- Avoid letting pots sit permanently in water.

If a potted tomato wilts every day despite regular watering, the plant may be rootbound, the pot may be too small, or the compost may have become difficult to re-wet. In that case, move the plant into a larger pot if it is still early enough in the season and the plant is otherwise healthy.

## Wilt diseases that watering will not fix

![Freshly cut tomato stem end placed in clear water for a bacterial ooze check.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-8-cut-stem-water-test.webp)




Some wilting is caused by organisms that block or damage the plant’s water-conducting tissues. These are more serious than ordinary water stress because the roots may be moist, but water still cannot move through the plant properly.

### Fusarium wilt

Fusarium wilt is one of the most common serious wilt diseases of tomatoes in many regions. It is caused by a soil-borne fungus that enters through the roots and moves into the plant’s vascular tissue. Vascular tissue is the plant’s internal water-and-nutrient transport system.

Typical clues include:

- lower leaves yellowing before the upper plant;
- wilting that may improve at night in the early stages;
- one side of a leaf, branch or plant looking worse than the other;
- stunted growth and reduced fruiting;
- brown streaking in the lower stem when it is cut lengthways or scraped.

Once a plant is clearly infected, watering, feeding or spraying will not cure it. Remove badly affected plants and keep infected roots and soil out of compost heaps. In future, choose varieties labelled with Fusarium resistance. Seed catalogues may use letters such as **F** or numbered Fusarium race resistance codes. Resistance is useful but not a guarantee in every garden because different races of the disease exist.

### Verticillium wilt

Verticillium wilt is another soil-borne vascular disease. It can look similar to Fusarium, but symptoms are often more uniform and may develop more slowly. Lower leaves yellow, wilt and die, and the plant gradually declines.

As with Fusarium, there is no quick home cure for an infected plant. Remove dead or severely affected plants, rotate away from tomatoes and related crops where possible, and choose resistant varieties marked **V** if Verticillium has been a problem.

### Bacterial wilt

Bacterial wilt can cause rapid collapse, sometimes while leaves remain green. It is more common in some warm, humid regions than others, so local climate matters. Early symptoms may appear during the hottest part of the day and may affect the top of the plant or just one side of a branch or leaflet.

A simple diagnostic clue is the **stem-in-water test**:

1. Cut a short section from the lower stem of a wilted plant.
2. Place the freshly cut end in a clear glass of clean water.
3. Watch for several minutes.
4. Milky white threads streaming from the cut end suggest bacterial ooze.

This test is not a substitute for a laboratory diagnosis, but it can help you decide whether to remove the plant and seek local advice. If bacterial wilt is suspected, do not move contaminated soil around the garden, and clean tools after use.

### Southern blight

Southern blight often shows up as sudden wilting and collapse, with damage concentrated at the soil line. Look at the base of the stem. You may see a dark brown canker, white fungal growth, or small tan, round structures that look a little like mustard seeds.

Remove affected plants, roots and nearby plant debris. Avoid burying infected material in the bed, and take care not to spread contaminated soil on tools, boots or stakes.

## Root and soil problems that make tomatoes wilt

### Root-knot nematodes

Root-knot nematodes are microscopic worms that feed on roots. You will not see the nematodes themselves without specialist equipment, but you may see their damage.

Above ground, affected tomatoes often look:

- stunted;
- pale green or yellowish;
- slow to grow;
- quick to wilt in warm weather;
- worse in one patch of the bed rather than evenly across the garden.

The key sign is underground. Carefully lift a failing plant and look for swollen, knobbly galls on the roots. Do not confuse these with the normal fine branching of healthy roots. If you are unsure, a local plant clinic or extension service can confirm the problem.

For future crops, use clean compost in containers or raised beds, rotate crops where space allows, remove roots after the season, and choose resistant varieties if root-knot nematodes are known to be present.

### Walnut toxicity

Tomatoes can be injured by juglone, a natural chemical produced by black walnut trees. Symptoms can include stunting, yellowing, wilting and eventual death. The risk is highest within the tree’s root zone and dripline, but roots can extend beyond the visible canopy.

If tomatoes repeatedly fail near a walnut tree but grow well elsewhere, move the bed or use large containers filled with clean compost and kept away from walnut leaves, nuts, roots and debris.

### Transplant shock and root disturbance

Newly planted tomatoes may wilt for a short time after transplanting, especially if they were set out on a hot, windy day or were not hardened off gradually. This should improve as roots establish.

Help transplants settle by:

- watering them well before and after planting;
- planting on a cloudy day or in late afternoon;
- protecting them from strong wind for a few days;
- avoiding fertiliser burn in the planting hole;
- installing stakes or cages at planting time so roots are not damaged later.

If a young plant wilts, blackens or collapses at the soil line, suspect cold damage, heat damage, damping-off or stem injury rather than ordinary transplant shock.

## Pests, viruses and chemical damage

### Stalk borers

If one stem or one entire plant suddenly wilts while neighbouring plants look fine, inspect the stem closely. Stalk borer larvae tunnel inside stems, interrupting water movement. The entry hole can be small and hard to find.

Cutting open a dead or failing stem may reveal tunnelling or a caterpillar. Remove and destroy badly affected stems or plants. Keeping tall weeds and grassy edges under control near the vegetable garden can reduce the chance of stalk borers moving into tomatoes.

### Tomato spotted wilt virus

Tomato spotted wilt virus can cause wilting, stunting, bronzed or discoloured foliage, and ring markings on fruit. It is spread by thrips, tiny insects that can move between weeds, ornamentals and vegetables.

There is no cure for a virus-infected tomato plant. Remove suspect plants, especially if symptoms are clear and the plant is declining. In areas where this virus is a regular problem, resistant varieties and good weed control are more useful than trying to treat sick plants.

### Herbicide damage

Tomatoes are very sensitive to some weedkillers. Drift from lawn sprays, vapour from poorly stored products, contaminated manure, contaminated compost, or lawn clippings treated with weedkiller can cause distorted growth.

Look for:

- twisted or curled new leaves;
- narrow, strap-like growth;
- small, distorted growing tips;
- symptoms appearing soon after nearby weedkiller use.

Mild damage may grow out if the source is removed and new growth becomes normal. Severe damage can permanently stunt the plant. Do not add lawn clippings or unknown manure around tomatoes unless you are confident they are free from persistent herbicide residues.

### Sap-sucking pests

Aphids, spider mites and other sap-sucking pests can weaken plants and cause curling, yellowing or a tired appearance. They are less likely to be the sole cause of sudden whole-plant wilt, but heavy infestations add stress, particularly in hot, dry weather.

Check leaf undersides and growing tips. Use a hand lens if you have one. Encourage natural predators, wash off small aphid colonies with water, and avoid unnecessary insecticide sprays that may worsen heat stress or harm beneficial insects.

## What to do today if your tomato plant is wilting

![Inspecting the stem base of a wilting tomato plant for problems at the soil line.](/images/tomato-problems/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting/why-are-my-tomato-plants-wilting-7-stem-base-inspection.webp)




Follow this order so you do not accidentally make the problem worse.

1. **Check soil moisture.** If dry, water slowly and deeply at the base. If wet, pause watering.
2. **Look for recovery.** Recheck in the evening and the next morning. Recovery suggests heat or temporary water stress.
3. **Inspect the stem base.** Look for cankers, collapse, white fungal growth, entry holes or unusual roots.
4. **Check the leaves.** Note whether yellowing begins low down, whether one side is worse, whether leaves stay green, or whether new growth is distorted.
5. **Inspect roots if the plant is failing.** Look for mushy roots, dry rootballs, root galls or severe root restriction.
6. **Cut a lower stem on a dying plant.** Brown internal streaking suggests vascular wilt. Milky streaming in water suggests bacterial wilt.
7. **Remove plants that are clearly diseased and declining.** Do not compost plants with suspected serious wilt diseases.
8. **Clean tools and supports.** Wash soil from tools, and disinfect blades after cutting diseased stems.
9. **Record where the plant grew.** This helps you rotate crops and choose resistant varieties next year.

If only one branch is affected, you may be able to prune it out if the rest of the plant is healthy and there are no signs of a systemic disease. If the whole plant is wilting and declining, removal is usually safer than trying to nurse it along.

## How to prevent wilting next season

You cannot prevent every tomato problem, but you can reduce the most common wilt risks.

### Water consistently

Water deeply rather than little and often. Mulch after the soil has warmed to conserve moisture and reduce temperature swings. Keep water at the base of the plant where possible, especially in humid weather, because wet foliage encourages some leaf diseases.

### Improve drainage

Avoid planting tomatoes in low, wet areas. If your soil stays soggy, use raised beds or large containers. Add composted organic matter to improve soil structure, but avoid fresh manure.

### Rotate tomato-family crops

Where space allows, avoid planting tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, aubergines and tomatillos in the same spot year after year. Rotation cannot instantly remove long-lived wilt pathogens, but it can reduce overall disease pressure and helps with many soil problems.

### Choose resistant varieties

If you have had wilt before, variety choice matters. Look for disease-resistance letters in seed catalogues and on plant labels. Common codes include **V** for Verticillium wilt and **F** for Fusarium wilt, sometimes with race numbers. If bacterial wilt, nematodes or tomato spotted wilt virus are common locally, look for varieties bred for those problems too.

Resistant does not mean indestructible. A resistant plant can still struggle in poor soil, drought, waterlogging, extreme heat or heavy disease pressure.

### Start with healthy plants

Buy sturdy, compact transplants with clean leaves and strong stems. Avoid plants with spots, yellowing, wilt or distorted new growth. If growing from seed, use clean containers and fresh seed compost.

### Keep tools, stakes and beds clean

Remove diseased plant debris at the end of the season. Wash soil from tools, cages and stakes. Do not move soil from a problem bed into clean containers.

### Reduce plant stress

A stressed tomato is less able to cope with heat, pests and disease. Give plants full sun, good spacing, support, steady watering and appropriate feeding. Avoid overfeeding with nitrogen, which encourages lush leafy growth that may be more vulnerable to some problems and slower to fruit.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Watering every wilted plant automatically.** A plant can wilt in wet soil if roots are damaged or oxygen-starved.
- **Assuming afternoon droop is always disease.** If the plant recovers overnight, heat stress is more likely.
- **Flooding a very dry plant.** Re-wet dry soil gradually so water soaks in rather than running off.
- **Composting diseased plants.** Serious wilt pathogens and infected debris can persist and spread.
- **Planting tomatoes in the same diseased bed every year.** Rotation, containers or raised beds may be needed.
- **Ignoring one-sided yellowing.** One-sided wilt can be a useful clue for Fusarium wilt.
- **Using lawn clippings around tomatoes after weedkiller use.** Herbicide residues can distort or damage plants.
- **Buying the biggest, lushest transplant.** Stocky, clean, unstressed plants usually establish better.
- **Applying sprays during hot weather without checking the label.** Some products can injure heat-stressed leaves.
- **Removing too many leaves in a heatwave.** Heavy pruning can expose fruit and stems to sunscald and add stress.

## FAQ

### Should I water tomato plants every day if they are wilting?

Not automatically. Check the soil first. If it is dry in the root zone, water deeply. If it is already wet, daily watering may make the problem worse by suffocating roots or encouraging root disease. Container tomatoes may need daily watering in hot weather, but in-ground plants usually need watering based on soil moisture, not the calendar.

### Why is my tomato plant wilting even though the soil is wet?

Wet-soil wilt often means the roots are not working properly. Causes include waterlogging, poor drainage, root rot, root damage, root-knot nematodes or vascular wilt diseases that block water movement inside the stem. Stop adding water, check drainage, and inspect the stem and roots.

### Can a wilted tomato plant recover?

Yes, if the cause is temporary heat stress, underwatering, mild transplant shock or a small amount of root disturbance. Recovery is less likely if the plant has Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, bacterial wilt, southern blight, severe nematode damage or a virus. A plant that stays wilted overnight and continues to decline is unlikely to recover fully.

### How can I tell heat wilt from disease wilt?

Heat wilt usually appears during the hottest part of the day and improves in the evening or by morning. Disease wilt often becomes progressively worse, may include yellowing, one-sided wilting, stem discolouration, green leaves collapsing, cankers, root galls or plant death despite moist soil.

### What does Fusarium wilt look like on tomatoes?

Fusarium wilt often starts with yellowing lower leaves, sometimes on one side of the plant or one side of a branch. The plant may wilt during the day and recover at night at first, then wilt permanently. Cutting or scraping the lower stem may show brown streaking in the vascular tissue.

### Should I remove a wilted tomato plant?

Remove it if it is clearly diseased, collapsing, not recovering overnight, or showing strong signs of vascular wilt, bacterial wilt, southern blight or virus infection. If it only droops in hot sun and recovers later, keep caring for it and reduce heat and water stress.

### Can too much fertiliser make tomatoes wilt?

Too much fertiliser, especially if placed close to roots, can stress or burn plants. Excess nitrogen can also encourage lush leafy growth that needs more water and may fruit poorly. If you suspect fertiliser burn, water the root zone thoroughly if drainage is good, avoid further feeding for a while, and watch for new healthy growth.

### Why is only one branch of my tomato plant wilting?

One wilting branch can be caused by physical damage, a stalk borer, Fusarium wilt beginning on one side, or a stem disease. Inspect the branch and main stem for cracks, holes, tunnelling, cankers and internal browning. Prune out a damaged branch if the rest of the plant is healthy, but remove the whole plant if systemic disease signs are present.

### Are wilt-resistant tomato varieties worth growing?

Yes, especially if you have had Fusarium or Verticillium wilt before. Look for resistance codes such as V and F on seed packets, catalogues or plant labels. Choose varieties suited to your local climate and known disease pressures. Resistance helps reduce losses, but good watering, drainage and hygiene are still important.

### Can I plant tomatoes again in soil where one wilted and died?

It depends on the cause. If the plant died from drought or heat, the soil may be fine once improved. If Fusarium, Verticillium, bacterial wilt, southern blight or nematodes are suspected, avoid replanting tomatoes or related crops in that spot if possible. Use rotation, clean containers, raised beds or resistant varieties next season.
