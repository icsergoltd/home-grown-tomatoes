Growing tomatoes in a greenhouse gives you a warmer, more protected space than an outdoor bed, which can mean earlier crops, a longer season and fewer weather-related problems. It also gives you more responsibility: under glass, tomatoes rely on you for watering, ventilation, feeding, support and often pollination.

**The short answer:** to grow tomatoes in a greenhouse, start seeds in late winter or early spring, plant sturdy young plants into large pots, grow bags, ring culture or greenhouse borders once conditions are warm, train cordon varieties up canes or strings, water evenly, feed once fruit begins to swell, ventilate daily, and gently shake open flower trusses to help fruit set.

A greenhouse does not need to be complicated. The most successful beginner routine is simple: keep plants warm but not overheated, keep the roots consistently moist, keep the leaves as dry and airy as possible, and check plants often so small problems do not become big ones.

## Why grow tomatoes in a greenhouse?

Tomatoes are tender, warm-season plants. A greenhouse helps by trapping warmth, sheltering plants from cold wind and heavy rain, and giving you more control over the crop.

The main advantages are:

- **Earlier planting and cropping** than outdoor plants in many climates.
- **A longer harvest season**, especially for tall cordon varieties.
- **Protection from heavy rain**, which can reduce some splash-spread leaf diseases.
- **Warmer night temperatures**, which help young plants establish.
- **Better control over watering**, feeding and support.

The trade-off is that a greenhouse can become too hot, too humid or too still. Those conditions can reduce fruit set and encourage problems such as leaf mould, grey mould, spider mites and blossom end rot. Good greenhouse tomato growing is mostly about balance.

## Best tomato types for greenhouse growing

Most greenhouse growers choose **cordon tomatoes**, also called **indeterminate tomatoes**. These grow as tall vines and continue producing new leaves, flowers and fruit along the main stem. They make excellent use of vertical greenhouse space.

Bush or determinate tomatoes can also work, especially in small greenhouses, but they spread more sideways and are usually less suited to string training.

### Good traits to look for

When buying seed or plants, look for varieties described as suitable for greenhouse, indoor or protected growing. Useful traits include:

- reliable fruit set under cover
- disease resistance, especially if you have grown tomatoes before
- crack resistance
- good flavour and fruit quality
- a manageable plant habit
- suitability for your greenhouse height

Cherry and small-fruited tomatoes are often easiest for beginners because they usually crop heavily and ripen readily. Large beefsteak tomatoes can be excellent, but they need steadier watering, stronger support and more patience.

If you are still choosing what to grow, this guide to [the best tomato varieties for gardens](/blog/growing-guides/best-varieties-for-gardens/) can help you compare different types before you fill the greenhouse.

## When to sow and plant greenhouse tomatoes

The exact timing depends on your local climate, whether your greenhouse is heated, and how bright your spring weather is.

For an unheated home greenhouse, a practical beginner schedule is:

| Job | Typical timing | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Sow seeds indoors | Late February to mid-March | Use warmth and good light to avoid weak, leggy seedlings. |
| Pot seedlings on | When the first true leaves appear | Handle seedlings by a leaf, not the delicate stem. |
| Move plants into the greenhouse by day | Spring, when conditions are mild | Bring them back in or protect them if nights are cold. |
| Plant in final positions | Once greenhouse conditions are reliably warm | Avoid planting into cold, wet compost or soil. |
| Start feeding | When first fruits begin to swell | Use a tomato feed or other high-potassium liquid feed. |
| Main harvest | Summer into early autumn | Greenhouse crops may start earlier than outdoor plants. |

Tomato seedlings germinate best with steady warmth. Once they appear, they need as much light as possible. Too much warmth with too little light produces tall, thin seedlings that are harder to manage later.

## How to prepare your greenhouse

![Washing tools and benches inside a home greenhouse before planting tomatoes.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-2-clean-before-planting.webp)


Before planting, give the greenhouse a simple reset. This is especially important if tomatoes, peppers, aubergines or potatoes have been grown there before, because related crops can share some pests and diseases.

### Clean before planting

Remove:

- old tomato stems and roots
- dropped fruit and leaves
- weeds
- used string or clips that cannot be cleaned
- old compost from diseased crops

Wash benches, pots, trays, canes and tools. If you reuse supports, clean them well before the new crop goes in. Good hygiene lowers the risk of carryover disease and gives you a cleaner start.

### Check ventilation and shade

Make sure vents, doors and louvres open easily. In hot weather, tomatoes under glass can overheat quickly. If your greenhouse regularly becomes very hot in summer, consider shade netting, blinds or a temporary wash-on shade product.

The aim is not to make the greenhouse dim. Tomatoes need strong light. The aim is to prevent damaging heat and reduce stress during the hottest part of the season.

### Plan your layout

Give each plant enough space for air to move around it. Crowding may seem efficient at planting time, but it makes watering, pruning, harvesting and disease control much harder later.

As a simple rule, allow about **45–60 cm between cordon tomato plants**, adjusting for the variety, container size and training system. Leaves should not be packed tightly against neighbouring plants once the crop is mature.

## Choosing pots, grow bags, borders or ring culture

You can grow tomatoes in a greenhouse in several ways. The best choice depends on your space, watering routine and whether your greenhouse has soil borders.

| Growing method | Best for | Main advantage | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large pots | Beginners and small greenhouses | Easy to position and replace compost each year | Pots dry quickly in hot weather. |
| Grow bags | Simple seasonal growing | Convenient and space-saving | Limited root space means watering must be steady. |
| Greenhouse border soil | Larger root run | More forgiving moisture levels | Rotate crops if possible and avoid disease build-up. |
| Ring culture | Keen greenhouse growers | Separates feeding compost from a moist aggregate bed | More setup and cleaning required. |

For most beginners, large pots or grow bags are easiest. Use a peat-free, good-quality compost and choose containers large enough to buffer plants against drying out. For more detail on container choice, see [the best pots for tomato plants](/blog/growing-guides/best-pots-for-tomato-plants/).

Grow bags are convenient in a greenhouse, but they can dry unevenly once the plants are large. If you choose this method, this guide to [growing tomatoes in grow bags](/blog/growing-guides/grow-tomatoes-in-grow-bags/) covers the setup in more detail.

### What is ring culture?

Ring culture is a greenhouse method where bottomless pots sit on a bed of moist aggregate, such as gravel. The compost in the ring holds nutrients around the plant, while roots grow down into the aggregate for moisture.

It can be useful where greenhouse border soil has disease problems or where you want a larger, more even root zone than a standard grow bag provides. However, it is more work to set up, keep moist and clean after the crop. Beginners do not need ring culture to grow good tomatoes, but it is an option once you are comfortable with the basics.

## How to plant tomatoes in a greenhouse

![Tomato plant being planted with part of the lower stem buried in compost.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-3-plant-deep-and-leave-lower-leaves.webp)

![Cane being inserted next to a newly planted greenhouse tomato.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-4-add-support-straight-away.webp)


Plant tomatoes when they are sturdy, well-rooted and the greenhouse is warm enough for steady growth. A good young plant is short and stocky, with healthy green leaves and no flowers dropping from stress.

### Step-by-step planting

1. **Water the plants first.** A moist rootball comes out of its pot more easily and settles better.
2. **Prepare the final position.** Fill pots, open grow bags, or prepare border soil with compost or well-rotted organic matter.
3. **Plant deeply.** Set the plant so part of the lower stem is buried, leaving the lowest leaves above the surface. Tomatoes can form roots from buried stem tissue.
4. **Firm gently.** Do not compact the compost hard, but remove large air gaps around the rootball.
5. **Water thoroughly.** Aim water at the compost, not the leaves.
6. **Add support straight away.** Insert a cane or install a string before the plant becomes floppy.

If planting into containers, one tomato per large pot is usually best. In a standard grow bag, two plants is a cautious beginner number because it gives each plant more root space and makes watering easier.

## Training and pruning greenhouse tomatoes

![Side shoot being pinched out on a cordon tomato with the main stem tied to a string.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-5-train-cordon-tie-loosely-remove-side-shoot.webp)


Training keeps greenhouse tomato plants upright, improves airflow and helps light reach the leaves and fruit. It also makes watering and harvesting much easier.

### Cordon tomatoes

Cordon tomatoes are usually grown as a single main stem. Support them with either:

- a strong cane pushed firmly into the pot or border soil, or
- a vertical string fixed to a safe overhead support and secured near the plant base.

As the plant grows, tie it loosely to the cane or twist the main stem carefully around the string. Do not tie stems tightly; they thicken as they grow.

Remove side-shoots regularly. These are the small shoots that appear in the joint between the main stem and a leaf. Pinch them out when they are small and soft. If they are allowed to grow, the plant becomes crowded, harder to support and less airy.

### Bush tomatoes

Bush tomatoes need much less pruning. Do not remove all their side-shoots, because these plants naturally crop on a branching framework. Give them space, support heavy stems if needed, and remove only damaged, yellowing or diseased foliage.

### Lower leaves

As plants mature, removing some lower leaves can improve airflow around the base of the crop. Be gradual. Remove yellowing, diseased or crowded lower leaves first, and avoid stripping the plant bare. The leaves are still the plant’s energy source.

### Stopping the plant

Late in the season, many growers remove the growing tip of cordon tomatoes so the plant puts energy into ripening existing fruit rather than making more flowers. In an unheated greenhouse, this is usually done when there is no realistic time left for new flowers to become ripe tomatoes.

## Watering and feeding greenhouse tomatoes

![Watering a greenhouse tomato at the soil surface near the stem base.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-6-water-at-base-not-leaves.webp)


Watering is one of the most important parts of growing tomatoes in a greenhouse. Under glass, rain cannot help you, and containers can dry out very quickly.

### How to water well

Aim for **consistent moisture**, not cycles of drought and flooding. Irregular watering can contribute to fruit splitting and blossom end rot.

Good habits include:

- watering at the base of the plant
- keeping leaves as dry as possible
- watering in the morning where practical
- checking containers daily in warm weather
- watering more than once a day during hot spells if pots or bags dry quickly
- using saucers only with care, so roots are not left sitting in stagnant water

In border soil, a mulch of compost or well-rotted organic matter can help conserve moisture. Keep mulch slightly away from the stem to reduce the risk of rotting at the base.

### Feeding greenhouse tomatoes

Tomatoes are hungry plants, but more feed is not always better. Too much nitrogen encourages lots of leafy growth at the expense of fruit.

A simple feeding routine is:

1. Use fresh compost or well-prepared soil at planting.
2. Water regularly while plants establish.
3. When the first fruits begin to swell, start feeding with a tomato fertiliser or high-potassium liquid feed.
4. Follow the product label rather than guessing stronger doses.
5. Never add fertiliser to bone-dry compost; water first if the root zone is dry.

Feeding every 10–14 days suits many container-grown plants, but the right frequency depends on compost, plant size, weather and the fertiliser used.

## Ventilation, temperature and humidity

![Opening greenhouse vents to ventilate tomato plants on a warm day.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-7-ventilate-open-door-and-louvre.webp)


A greenhouse protects tomatoes, but it can also trap heat and humidity. Both matter.

### Ventilate daily

Open vents and doors regularly to:

- reduce excess heat
- lower humidity
- improve air movement
- reduce condensation
- allow some pollinating insects inside
- replenish fresh air around the crop

Still, humid air encourages fungal problems. It also makes pollen less reliable. On mild days, ventilate early rather than waiting until the greenhouse is already hot.

### Avoid overheating

Tomatoes like warmth, but very high temperatures can reduce fruit set, stress plants and worsen watering problems. If the greenhouse overheats:

- open doors and roof vents
- use shading during the hottest weeks
- water early so plants are not drought-stressed by midday
- damp down paths only when extra humidity will not linger late into the evening
- improve air movement with a small greenhouse fan if safe and practical

### Avoid cold shock

Young plants dislike cold nights. If you plant too early into an unheated greenhouse, growth may stall and leaves may show stress colours or curling. Wait until conditions are reliably warm, or use fleece and temporary protection during cold snaps.

## How to pollinate greenhouse tomatoes

![Gently shaking a greenhouse tomato flower truss to help pollination.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-8-shake-flower-truss-midday.webp)


Tomato flowers are self-pollinating, which means each flower has the parts needed to set fruit. Outdoors, wind and insects shake the flowers and help pollen move. In a still greenhouse, that movement may be missing.

Signs of poor pollination include:

- flowers dropping without forming fruit
- small or misshapen fruit
- poor fruit set during hot or very humid weather

For a home greenhouse, the simplest method is to gently tap or shake open flower trusses around the middle of the day, when the air is usually warmer and less humid. You can also lightly flick the support cane or use a soft electric toothbrush against the flower stem for a second or two. Do not press into the flower itself or damage developing fruit.

Repeat this several times a week while new flowers are opening. Bumblebee hives are used in larger commercial greenhouses, but they are usually unnecessary and impractical for a small domestic greenhouse.

## Common greenhouse tomato problems

Check plants often, especially during hot, humid or dull weather. Look at the undersides of leaves, the lower canopy, flowers and developing fruit.

| Problem | What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blossom end rot | Dark, sunken patch at the flower end of fruit | Irregular water uptake, often in pots or grow bags | Keep compost evenly moist; use larger containers or automatic watering if needed. Affected fruit will not recover. |
| Leaf curl | Leaves roll upwards, often in hot weather | Heat, water stress or heavy pruning | Check moisture, improve ventilation and avoid drastic pruning. |
| Leaf mould | Yellow patches on upper leaves, mouldy growth beneath | Humid, still greenhouse air | Remove affected leaves, increase ventilation and avoid wet foliage. |
| Grey mould | Fuzzy grey-brown mould on dead flowers, leaves, stems or fruit | Cool, damp, poorly ventilated conditions | Remove dead tissue, improve airflow, avoid wounding and keep plants dry. |
| Powdery mildew | Pale or white powdery patches on leaves | Humid conditions and poor airflow | Remove badly affected leaves and improve spacing and ventilation. |
| Spider mites | Fine speckling, bronzing, webbing on leaf undersides | Hot, dry greenhouse conditions | Check early, remove badly affected leaves and use suitable biological controls or approved treatments if needed. |
| Aphids | Clusters of small insects on soft growth, sticky honeydew | Pest build-up under cover | Squash small colonies, encourage predators and avoid overfeeding with nitrogen. |
| Fruit splitting | Cracks in ripening fruit | Watering fluctuations or sudden water uptake | Water more evenly and harvest fruit as it colours. |

### Prevention is easier than cure

Most greenhouse tomato problems are reduced by the same good habits:

- space plants well
- water at the base
- ventilate every day when weather allows
- remove dead leaves and dropped fruit
- keep weeds down
- clean supports and tools between crops
- avoid handling wet plants
- inspect plants several times a week

If a plant is badly diseased, remove affected material promptly. Do not compost diseased tomato debris unless your composting system reliably gets hot enough to break down plant pathogens.

## Harvesting greenhouse tomatoes

![Picking a ripe greenhouse tomato that feels slightly softer than hard green fruit.](/images/growing-guides/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse/how-to-grow-tomatoes-in-a-greenhouse-9-harvest-ripe-colour-slightly-soft.webp)


Pick tomatoes when they have developed good colour and feel slightly softer than hard green fruit. Regular picking encourages you to notice problems early and stops overripe fruit from splitting or rotting on the plant.

At the end of the season, remove remaining fruit before cold, damp conditions spoil it. Mature green tomatoes can often ripen indoors at room temperature. They do not need bright sunlight to ripen; warmth and time matter more.

After the final harvest, clear the crop thoroughly. Remove old roots where practical, take down string, clean reusable supports, and dispose of diseased leaves or fruit. A clean greenhouse in autumn makes next year’s tomato crop much easier.

## Common mistakes to avoid

### Planting too early

A greenhouse feels warm on a sunny day but can still become cold at night. Planting into cold compost slows growth and can stress young tomatoes.

### Using containers that are too small

Small pots dry quickly and restrict roots. This makes watering harder and increases the risk of blossom end rot and poor cropping.

### Crowding plants

Too many plants create a dense, humid canopy. You may get more leaves, but not necessarily more good fruit.

### Forgetting to pollinate

In a still greenhouse, flowers may need gentle shaking. If plants look healthy but fruit set is poor, pollination conditions may be the issue.

### Watering randomly

Letting grow bags dry out and then soaking them is a common route to split fruit and blossom end rot. Aim for steady moisture.

### Overfeeding before fruiting

Heavy feeding too early can make plants leafy and vigorous but slow to crop. Start regular tomato feed when fruit begins to swell.

### Poor ventilation

Keeping the greenhouse closed to “hold in warmth” can backfire. Stale, humid air encourages disease and can interfere with pollination.

### Removing too many leaves at once

Leaf removal can improve airflow, but severe stripping weakens the plant and exposes fruit to sudden sun scorch. Remove leaves gradually and sensibly.

## FAQ

### Can you grow tomatoes in a greenhouse all year?

In a heated, lit and well-managed greenhouse, tomatoes can be grown for a much longer season. In a typical unheated home greenhouse, they are usually grown from spring to autumn. Winter light and temperature are often too low for productive growth without extra equipment.

### Do greenhouse tomatoes need bees?

No. Tomato flowers can self-pollinate, but they still need movement to release pollen. In a small greenhouse, gently tapping or vibrating open flower trusses several times a week is usually enough.

### Should I mist greenhouse tomato flowers?

Light misting is sometimes suggested to help pollen transfer, but be careful. Frequent wet foliage and high humidity can encourage disease. For most beginners, improving ventilation and gently shaking flower trusses is a safer first step.

### How often should I water tomatoes in a greenhouse?

There is no fixed schedule. Small plants in cool weather may need little water, while large plants in pots or grow bags may need watering every day, and sometimes more than once a day in hot weather. Check the compost regularly and aim to keep it evenly moist.

### Should I grow greenhouse tomatoes in pots or grow bags?

Both can work. Pots are often easier to manage because you can choose a generous size and replace compost each year. Grow bags are convenient but have limited root volume, so watering must be more consistent.

### Why are my greenhouse tomato leaves turning yellow?

Yellow leaves can be caused by natural ageing, lack of nutrients, overwatering, underwatering, poor roots, pests or disease. Start by checking the oldest lower leaves, compost moisture, feeding routine and airflow. Remove yellow leaves if they are dying or diseased.

### Why are flowers falling off my greenhouse tomatoes?

Flower drop is often linked to poor pollination, very high temperatures, low light, water stress or high humidity. Ventilate well, keep watering even, avoid overheating and gently shake open flower trusses around midday.

### When should I stop greenhouse tomato plants?

Stop cordon tomatoes late in the season when new flowers no longer have time to develop into ripe fruit. Remove the growing tip above the last useful truss so the plant can focus on swelling and ripening existing tomatoes.

## Final thoughts

To grow tomatoes in a greenhouse successfully, focus on the basics: warmth without overheating, steady moisture, good support, regular pruning, daily ventilation and simple hand pollination. You do not need a commercial setup or complicated equipment. A clean greenhouse, healthy young plants and a consistent care routine are enough to produce a generous crop of home-grown tomatoes under glass.
